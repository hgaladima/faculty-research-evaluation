# Developing a Comprehensive Faculty Research and Scholarship Evaluation Algorithm

## 1. Objective

I would like you to act as an expert in **faculty performance evaluation, academic research assessment, higher education administration, and quantitative scoring methodology**.

Your task is to develop a comprehensive, objective, transparent, and evidence-based algorithm for evaluating the annual **research and scholarly productivity of faculty members in a School of Public Health**.

The primary goal is to determine whether a faculty member:

1. **Does Not Meet Expectations**
2. **Meets Expectations**
3. **Exceeds Expectations** (an additional category that should be explored and justified)

The evaluation should generate a **numerical research performance score (0–100)** and an overall research performance classification.

The algorithm should recognize differences in scholarly productivity, research quality, impact, and effort while remaining fair across faculty ranks, disciplines, research areas, and career stages.

## 3. Core Research Evaluation Domains

The evaluation should incorporate three primary domains:

### A. Peer-Reviewed Publications and Scholarly Outputs

The School expects faculty to produce a **minimum of two peer-reviewed publications annually**.

However, the scoring system should consider both the quantity and quality of publications.

Potential evaluation criteria include:

**Publication quantity**
- Number of peer-reviewed publications.
- Meeting the minimum expectation of two publications.
- Additional publications beyond the minimum.
- Publication productivity over the evaluation period.

**Publication quality and impact**
- Journal reputation and standing within the discipline.
- Journal impact factor, when appropriate.
- Journal quartile rankings (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4).
- Field-normalized citation impact, when available.
- Indexing in reputable databases.
- Selectivity and rigor of the peer-review process.
- Evidence of scholarly influence or real-world impact.

**Faculty contribution**
- First authorship.
- Corresponding authorship.
- Senior or last authorship.
- Collaborative or coauthored publications.
- Substantial intellectual contributions.
- Student-led publications supervised by the faculty member.

**Other scholarly outputs**

Consider additional scholarly products identified in the institutional documents, such as:
- Books and scholarly book chapters.
- Technical reports.
- Public health research reports.
- Methodological papers.
- Statistical software, analytical tools, or research databases.
- Publicly available research datasets.
- Policy briefs.
- Other recognized forms of scholarship.

Develop a system that appropriately rewards these activities without automatically treating all scholarly outputs as equivalent to peer-reviewed journal publications.

**Important:** A faculty member publishing four articles in highly regarded journals should generally receive a higher publication score than someone publishing only two comparable articles. However, journal impact factor alone should not determine research quality.

### B. Grants, Contracts, and External Research Funding

Faculty are expected to demonstrate active engagement in pursuing research funding.

The evaluation should distinguish between **grant-seeking activity and successful funding acquisition**.

Consider the following:

**Grant applications**
- Number of proposals submitted.
- Role in the proposal (PI, Co-PI, Co-I, collaborator).
- Competitive versus noncompetitive funding.
- Internal versus external funding.
- Federal, foundation, state, local, and international funding sources.
- Complexity and competitiveness of the application.
- Resubmissions and revised proposals.
- Proposal development and collaborative grant leadership.

**Grant awards**
- Number of grants awarded.
- Total awarded funding.
- Faculty-specific funding allocation.
- Role and level of responsibility.
- Grant duration.
- Competitive prestige.
- Research contracts and funded evaluation projects.
- Continuation or renewal awards.

**Important considerations**

The algorithm should reward the effort involved in preparing and submitting competitive proposals, even when they are not funded.

However, **successfully obtaining a competitive external research grant should generally receive more points than submitting an unsuccessful proposal**.

The algorithm should also account for differences in funding opportunities across disciplines.

For example, obtaining a $50,000 competitive research grant as PI may represent greater individual scholarly leadership than participating as a minor collaborator on a $2 million grant.

Avoid double-counting the same grant across multiple evaluation years.

### C. Scientific Presentations and Research Dissemination

The School expects faculty to participate in scholarly presentations and dissemination activities.

Develop a scoring system that differentiates presentations based on their scholarly rigor, visibility, and impact.

Consider:

**Conference scope**
- International conferences.
- National conferences.
- Regional conferences.
- State and local conferences.
- Institutional research symposia.

**Conference reputation and selectivity**
- Prestigious conferences organized by recognized professional associations.
- Conferences with competitive peer-reviewed abstract selection.
- Conferences with less selective abstract review.
- Conferences without meaningful peer review.
- Conferences with questionable academic credibility.

**Presentation type**
- Invited keynote presentations.
- Invited plenary presentations.
- Invited scientific talks.
- Peer-reviewed oral presentations.
- Peer-reviewed poster presentations.
- Panel discussions.
- Research workshops.
- Student presentations supervised by faculty.

**Faculty contribution**
- Presenting author.
- Coauthor.
- Session organizer or scientific chair.
- Faculty mentor supervising student research presentations.

For example:

A peer-reviewed oral presentation at a highly respected international scientific conference should generally receive more points than a poster presentation at a regional conference with limited abstract review.

However, **conference quality and scholarly rigor should carry more weight than geographic scope alone**. An excellent national conference may be more prestigious than a poorly organized international conference.

## 4. Additional Scholarly Contributions

Review the institutional documents to identify other activities that may qualify as research or scholarship.

Examples may include:

- Research mentorship of doctoral and master's students.
- Dissertation research supervision resulting in scholarly products.
- Interdisciplinary research collaborations.
- Research leadership and laboratory development.
- Development of new statistical or methodological approaches.
- Translation of research findings into public health policy or practice.
- Community-engaged research.
- Research awards and scholarly recognition.
- Contributions to scientific guidelines or public health recommendations.

Determine which activities should receive direct points, bonus points, or qualitative recognition.

Avoid double-counting activities already credited under publications, grants, or presentations.

Also distinguish research scholarship from activities that should primarily be evaluated under teaching or service.

## 5. Develop a Quantitative Scoring System

Create a comprehensive scoring system using a **0–100 scale**.

Propose and justify an appropriate distribution of points across the three primary research domains.

For example, an initial model might allocate:

| Research domain | Proposed maximum points |
|---|---:|
| Publications and scholarly outputs | 50 |
| Grants and contracts | 35 |
| Presentations and dissemination | 15 |
| **Total** | **100** |

These weights are illustrative. Evaluate whether alternative distributions would be more appropriate based on the institutional expectations.

Consider whether additional scholarly contributions should be incorporated within these domains or assigned a separate, capped bonus.

### Scoring principles

The algorithm should:

1. Award points for meeting minimum expectations.
2. Award additional points for exceeding expectations.
3. Reward scholarly quality and impact, not merely quantity.
4. Recognize meaningful research contributions and leadership.
5. Avoid excessive dependence on a single metric.
6. Prevent double-counting.
7. Establish reasonable caps on individual activities.
8. Ensure that faculty meeting all established minimum expectations can achieve a **Meets Expectations** classification.
9. Ensure that outstanding achievements can produce an **Exceeds Expectations** classification.
10. Prevent exceptionally high performance in one domain from automatically masking substantial deficiencies in other required domains.

Develop explicit mathematical formulas for calculating the scores.

## 6. Establish Evaluation Thresholds

Recommend clear and defensible thresholds for the three performance categories.

For example:

| Overall research score | Proposed classification |
|---|---|
| Below 70 | Does Not Meet Expectations |
| 70–89 | Meets Expectations |
| 90–100 | Exceeds Expectations |

These thresholds are preliminary and should be evaluated rather than automatically adopted.

**An important requirement is that a faculty member who fulfills all institutional minimum research expectations should receive a Meets Expectations classification.**

Explore whether the final classification should depend on both:

- The numerical score.
- Minimum performance requirements across publications, grants, and presentations.

For example, consider whether someone with no publications but several large grants should qualify as meeting expectations.

Develop appropriate decision rules and exceptions.

## 7. Consider Important Faculty Circumstances

The algorithm should be fair and flexible enough to account for:

- Assistant, associate, and full professors.
- Tenure-track and tenured faculty.
- Research-intensive versus teaching-intensive appointments.
- Faculty with different assigned research effort.
- Joint appointments.
- New faculty members.
- Approved sabbaticals and research leave.
- Medical, parental, or other approved leave.
- Differences in funding availability across research disciplines.
- Multiyear research projects.
- Publication delays and grant review timelines.
- Faculty transitioning between research areas.
- Collaborative versus independently led research.
- Differences in research infrastructure and available institutional support.

Consider whether a one-year evaluation should be supplemented by a rolling three-year assessment to avoid unfairly penalizing faculty because of publication or funding cycles.

Explain when adjustments should be made and how to preserve transparency and consistency.

8. Faculty obtaining a major competitive federal grant as PI.
9. Faculty participating as Co-I on several large grants.
10. Faculty with several conference presentations but limited publications.
11. Faculty presenting at prestigious international conferences.
12. Faculty with substantial research mentorship and student publications.
13. Faculty publishing interdisciplinary research with extensive coauthorship.
14. Faculty producing high-impact public health policy research.
15. Faculty with minimal research activity.
16. Early-career faculty building a research program.
17. Faculty returning from approved leave.
18. Faculty with strong multiyear research productivity but a low-output evaluation year.
19. Faculty with exceptional performance across all three research domains.

For each scenario, calculate:

- Publication score.
- Grant score.
- Presentation score.
- Additional scholarly contribution score, if applicable.
- Total research score.
- Final evaluation classification.
- Brief explanation of the outcome.

Identify any counterintuitive or unfair classifications and refine the algorithm accordingly.

## 9. Conduct Sensitivity and Fairness Analyses

Evaluate how the results change under alternative scoring assumptions.

For example:

- What happens if publication weight increases from 50% to 60%?
- What happens if grant weight increases?
- What happens if a faculty member publishes one additional article?
- How much does obtaining a grant change the overall score?
- Does the algorithm unfairly favor faculty in highly funded disciplines?
- Can someone exceed expectations through publication quantity alone?
- Can one exceptionally prestigious publication outweigh several lower-impact publications?
- Can a faculty member meet expectations without obtaining a grant despite demonstrating substantial grant-seeking effort?

Assess the robustness, fairness, and interpretability of the proposed scoring system.

Recommend adjustments where necessary.

## 12. Additional Instructions

Approach this task as if you were developing a faculty research evaluation framework for consideration by a School of Public Health's faculty evaluation committee.

The model should be:

- **Evidence-based:** Grounded in recognized principles of responsible research assessment.
- **Transparent:** Every point should have a clear justification.
- **Objective:** Minimize unnecessary subjective judgments.
- **Fair:** Account for differences in research disciplines, roles, and circumstances.
- **Flexible:** Allow reasonable adjustments without compromising consistency.
- **Practical:** Avoid creating an overly complicated system that is difficult to administer.
- **Defensible:** Produce classifications that can be explained to faculty and academic leadership.
- **Reproducible:** The same inputs should generate the same scores and classifications.

Where relevant, consult established frameworks for responsible research assessment, such as the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA), the Leiden Manifesto, and the Coalition for Advancing Research Assessment (CoARA).

**Do not simply propose a scoring table. Critically evaluate the assumptions, test the algorithm against realistic faculty scenarios, identify weaknesses, and refine the model before presenting your final recommendation.**

The ultimate goal is to create a **fair, comprehensive, transparent, and quantitatively defensible faculty research evaluation system** that recognizes both expected scholarly productivity and exceptional research contributions.
